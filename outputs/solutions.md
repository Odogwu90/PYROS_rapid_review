# Solutions list: what helps skilled migrants who must restart their careers

**Draft v0.1, 2026-09-12. Companion to `outputs/report.md`.**

Rules applied (CLAUDE.md rule 6): every evidence-based item below states only what the included studies support and names the study ID. Evidence strength is graded in plain words: *one small pilot*, *one cross-sectional study*, *two or more studies*. Items in the "Lived-experience advice" boxes are to be written by Dr. Okpara and are labelled as personal experience, not research findings.

What the evidence cannot yet say: no included study compared established professionals with recent graduates, so nothing here is specific to one group over the other.

---

## A. For governments and licensing bodies

| # | Solution | What the studies show | Evidence strength |
|---|----------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| A1 | Shorten and clarify the licensing and credential-recognition process | The waiting period before re-entry to the profession was described as the most distressing phase; Syrian doctors in Germany reported a long, changing and opaque process and used the words depressed and in despair (S117). Migrants awaiting accreditation in Norway reported symptoms of depression and stress before a placement began (S139). | Two studies (one qualitative, one pilot) |
| A2 | Provide one official, up-to-date information source on requirements, documents and waiting times | Interviewees relied on peers and social media because official information was scarce or contradictory (S117). | One qualitative study |
| A3 | Fund bridging placements with same-profession mentors while accreditation is pending | A six-month placement as assistant teachers improved general mental health (GHQ-12) and well-being; the anxiety-depression score did not change (S139). | One small non-randomised pilot (21 participants) |
| A4 | Track and reduce qualification mismatch among skilled migrants | Working below qualification predicted higher PHQ-9 scores among Russian migrants; the Ukrainian sample, with far more mismatch, had a higher proportion above the cut-off (S172). A fall of three or more rungs in perceived social standing tripled the odds of a major depressive episode (S159). | Two cross-sectional studies |

## B. For employers and professional bodies

| # | Solution | What the studies show | Evidence strength |
|---|----------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| B1 | Offer permanent rather than fixed-term contracts and give migrant staff real influence over their work | Fixed-term contracts and low influence at work were linked to distress among immigrant homecare nurses (S039). | One cross-sectional study |
| B2 | Build good relationships between migrant staff, colleagues and supervisors | Relationships with colleagues and superiors were protective (S039). Mentors were the main source of support in the placement pilot (S139). | Two studies |
| B3 | Watch working hours and language load | Long working hours and language barriers predicted higher depressive-symptom scores among Korean immigrant workers, including professionals (S107). | One cross-sectional study |
| B4 | Respond to applications, including for unpaid internships | Unanswered applications and refusals of unpaid internships were described as humiliating by experienced doctors (S117). | One qualitative study |
| B5 | Train hiring staff on religious and cultural diversity | All three women interviewed reported discrimination linked to wearing a hijab in job interviews (S117). | One qualitative study, three participants |

## C. For skilled migrants themselves

| # | Solution | What the studies show | Evidence strength |
|---|----------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| C1 | Seek any structured contact with your profession early, even unpaid or academic | Placement participants described feeling "like myself again" and regained confidence and a plan for licensing (S139). | One small pilot |
| C2 | Hold on to your professional identity during the transition | Refugee physicians in Turkey who saw themselves as physicians rather than refugees were described as doing better; planning to return home went with higher depressive-symptom scores (S058). | One cross-sectional study |
| C3 | Prefer active, problem-focused coping over resignation | Resignation and passivity predicted higher depressive-symptom scores in both forced-migrant samples; active coping was linked to lower scores in the Ukrainian sample (S172). | One cross-sectional study |
| C4 | Use peer networks for practical information, but treat them as a stopgap | Syrian doctors depended on peer and social-media networks to navigate licensing (S117). | One qualitative study |
| C5 | Learn the local language | Language barriers predicted higher scores in Toronto (S107) and lower language knowledge went with more symptoms among Ukrainian women in Poland (S172). | Two cross-sectional studies |

## D. For host communities and families

| # | Solution | What the studies show | Evidence strength |
|---|----------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| D1 | Welcoming attitudes matter | Negative attitudes of the local population were the strongest predictor of depressive-symptom scores among Ukrainian women in Poland (S172). | One cross-sectional study |
| D2 | Family recognition of the migrant's work restores standing | Participants, especially women, reported that their children saw them differently once they had a workplace (S139). | One small pilot |
| D3 | Enable family visits | Highly educated Iranians in the United States described years of enforced family separation as a major source of distress (S199). | One qualitative study |

---

## What the studies did NOT show (rule 4)

- No evidence that immigrant professionals who are already practising have more depressive symptoms than local colleagues (S039, S075) or than other immigrant workers (S107).
- No evidence yet that any intervention reduces depressive-symptom scores specifically; the one pilot improved general mental health and well-being only (S139).
- No evidence on whether recent graduates fare better or worse than established professionals.
