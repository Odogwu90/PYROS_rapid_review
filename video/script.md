# Video script: "The doctor who drives a taxi"

**Format:** narrator voice over graphics. **Target length:** 12 minutes (roughly 1,700 spoken words at a calm pace). **Draft v0.1, 2026-09-12.**

Rules for this script (README and CLAUDE.md): plain language for a worldwide general audience; no jargon; no statistics, p-values or scale names on screen. Every fact spoken traces to a study in `extraction/extraction.csv` or a retained qualitative study; the source column is for the producer and is never shown on screen. Where the evidence is thin, the script says so.

Graphics style note: simple flat illustrations, one idea per screen, warm colours, no charts with numbers. Text on screen is limited to short phrases.

---

## Scene 4. Good news: once you are back in your field (3:30 to 5:00)

| Voice-over | On screen | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Let us start with the good news. In Germany, researchers compared nurses who had migrated with nurses born in the country, all doing the same home-care work. They asked about low mood, poor sleep, loss of interest, the signs of depressive symptoms. | Two nurses side by side, one with a small suitcase icon. A German map outline. | S039 |
| The migrant nurses were no worse off than their local colleagues. About one in five in each group had troubling symptoms. The same number. | Two identical rows of five nurse icons, one in each row shaded. Text: **About the same.** | S039 (21.9% vs 21.4%) |
| In Finland, another study asked the same kind of question of foreign-born and Finnish nurses. Again, no difference. | Finland map outline. Two nurses, an equals sign. | S075 |
| In Toronto, Canada, researchers looked at Korean immigrants in four kinds of work: professionals, small business owners, office workers and manual workers. The professionals had the lowest levels of depressive symptoms of all four. | Four figures on a gentle slope; the professional stands highest. Text: **Professionals: lowest symptoms.** | S107 |
| Notice what these three studies have in common. The people were already working in their field, or at least working. They had made it through the door. | A door, open, light coming through. | Report section 8 |
| The message is not that migration is painless. The message is that once a skilled migrant is practising again, they look much like everyone else. | Text: **Once you are through the door, you look like everyone else.** | Report key message 2 |

## Scene 5. The hard part: the waiting room (5:00 to 6:45)

| Voice-over | On screen | Source |
|---|---|---|
| So where does the pain live? The studies point to one place: the gap between arriving and working at your level. | A waiting room with a clock. The hands move fast. | Report section 8 |
| In the United States, researchers asked more than three thousand immigrants from Latin America and Asia a simple question. Picture a ladder of society. Where did you stand at home? Where do you stand now? | A ladder. A figure on a high rung, then the same figure on a lower rung. | S159 |
| People who had dropped three or more rungs were three times as likely to have had a serious episode of depression in the past year, compared with people whose position had not changed. That held true whatever their education, income or years in the country. | The figure sliding down the ladder; a shadow grows around them. Text: **The bigger the fall, the greater the risk.** | S159 (OR 3.0, 1.3 to 6.6) |
| A more recent study followed people forced to leave Ukraine and Russia after the war began. Almost all were university educated. Among the Ukrainian women in Poland, only about one in four were working in a job that matched their qualification. Among the Russians, mostly in Serbia, about seven in ten were. | Two groups of ten figures. In the first, about three wear a badge; in the second, about seven do. | S172 (26.8% vs 69.8%) |
| The group with far more people working below their level also had far more people with depressive symptoms. More than half in one group, under four in ten in the other. And in the Russian group, working below your qualification was itself linked to worse symptoms. | The same two groups; more figures shaded in the first group. Text: **Working below your level hurts.** | S172 (55.6% vs 38.7%; qualification mismatch predictor) |
| These two groups differed in many ways, so this is a clue, not a proof. But it points the same direction as the ladder. | Text: **A clue, not a proof.** | Report section 8 |
| And in one hospital sample in Turkey, among five hundred and seventy Syrian doctors who had fled the war, about one in ten had moderate or severe depressive symptoms. Those who were planning to go back, and those without experience in primary care, were doing worse. Those who still saw themselves as doctors first, not as refugees, were doing better. | A doctor's white coat on a hanger; a hand reaches for it. Text: **"I am still a doctor."** | S058 |

## Scene 7. What helps (8:15 to 10:15)

| Voice-over | On screen | Source |
|---|---|---|
| So what helps? The studies give us five answers, and we will be honest about how strong each one is. | Five empty circles appear. | Solutions list |
| One. Getting back into your field, even before the licence arrives. In Norway, doctors, pharmacists, a dentist, a nurse and a radiographer who were still waiting for accreditation spent six months as assistant teachers in university health courses, each with a mentor from their own profession. | A migrant in a lecture hall, a mentor beside them. Norway map outline. Circle 1 fills. | S139 |
| Their general mental health and their sense of well-being improved. Their specific depressive symptoms did not change, and the study was small, so we call this promising, not proven. But listen to what they said: "I felt like myself again." | Quote card: **"I felt like myself again."** Text: **Promising, not proven.** | S139 |
| Something else happened in those families. Mothers said their children began to speak of them differently. "My mother works at the university." A door pass, a reason to leave the house, a walk with colleagues at lunch. Small things that rebuild a person. | A child holding up a parent's university access card. | S139 (page 8, family pride; physical activity) |
| Two. Good colleagues and a secure job. Among migrant nurses in Germany, good relationships with co-workers and managers protected against distress. Short-term contracts and having no say at work did the opposite. | Two nurses sharing coffee. A contract with the word "permanent". Circle 2 fills. | S039 |
| Three. Holding on to who you are. The Syrian doctors in Turkey who kept their identity as physicians were doing better. | The white coat again, now worn. Circle 3 fills. | S058 |
| Four. Language. In Toronto and in Poland, language difficulties went with more symptoms. Every hour spent learning the local language is an investment in your mind, not only your career. | An open language textbook. Circle 4 fills. | S107, S172 |
| Five. Welcome. Among the Ukrainian women in Poland, the single strongest predictor of depressive symptoms was how the local population treated them. Not their education. Not their housing. Whether they felt wanted. | An open door with a neighbour waving. Circle 5 fills. Text: **Welcome is medicine.** | S172 |
| And one more, about how we cope. In both the Ukrainian and Russian groups, people who said "I give up, I do not know what to do" had more symptoms. People who said "I get organised and do my best" had fewer. That is one study, and coping is easier to advise than to do. But it is worth knowing. | Two figures: one seated, head down; one standing, writing a list. | S172 (Table 3, regression; solutions C3) |

- Numbers spoken aloud are rounded in words ("about one in five", "three times as likely"). No numbers, scale names or study names appear on screen except the paper-count icons in Scene 3.
- Quotes on screen are verbatim from the studies (S117 page 8, S139 page 8) and are shown without names.
- Country outlines are used only where the study took place; no flags.
- Add subtitles in English first; the script avoids idioms to ease translation.
